# approved-claims.md

> **Status:** Working draft v1. Do not ship public-facing copy that depends on this file until a qualified Dutch regulatory consultant (or the regulatory advisor at your chosen halal-certification body) has reviewed and signed off on it.
> **Authority:** EU Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 (Nutrition and Health Claims) and Commission Regulation (EU) No 432/2012 (the list of permitted health claims), plus subsequent amendments. The source of truth is the EU Register of nutrition and health claims at the Commission's Food and Feed Information Portal — not this file, not training data, not competitor copy.
> **Scope:** EU/NL market (active). UK and US claim sets live in separate files (`markets/uk.md`, `markets/us.md`) and are NOT interchangeable — Brexit-era FSA wording differs in detail, and US FDA "structure/function" claims follow a different framework entirely.
> **Loaded by:** every agent that produces public-facing copy — Customer Support, Creative Intelligence, Email Marketing, Store Ops, Content Production. The agent MUST load this file before drafting any copy that names an ingredient or describes a benefit. Drafts that bypass this file are unsafe by definition.

---

## 0. The two rules every agent obeys

**Rule 1 — Quote, don't paraphrase.** Authorised claims must be used in wording that has the same meaning for the consumer as the official wording. The safest pattern is to use the exact wording in this file verbatim. If you must rephrase for tone, the rephrasing cannot strengthen, broaden, or shift the meaning. When in doubt, quote.

**Rule 2 — If it isn't in this file, you cannot say it.** No exceptions for "everyone else says it," "the competitor's site says it," "it's only in an ad caption," "it's framed as an opinion," or "it's a customer testimonial we're quoting." The Regulation applies to all commercial communications including ads, emails, social posts, product pages, FAQs, and republished testimonials. If a customer reviewed the product saying it "cured" something, you do not publish that review.

If an agent finds itself reframing a request to make it compliant ("but if I just soften it…"), the agent MUST stop and route to `#approvals` for a human read. That reframing instinct is the signal to ask, not to proceed.

## 1. Collagen itself — what you CANNOT claim

This is the single most important section in the file. **EFSA has not authorised any health claim for collagen, hydrolysed collagen, collagen peptides, marine collagen, or bovine collagen in the EU.** The European Commission has explicitly considered collagen-related submissions and the result is the same: no authorised skin claim, no authorised hair claim, no authorised nail claim, no authorised joint claim, no authorised "beauty-from-within" claim, no authorised wrinkle/elasticity/hydration claim.

This is not a grey area. It is a settled regulatory position.

### Forbidden claims about collagen (non-exhaustive list)

The following kinds of statements MUST NOT appear anywhere in any commercial communication for the product:

- "Improves skin elasticity / firmness / hydration"
- "Reduces wrinkles / fine lines / signs of aging"
- "Stronger hair / nails / thicker hair / faster nail growth"
- "Supports joint health / joint comfort / joint mobility / cartilage"
- "Anti-aging / beauty from within / youthful glow / luminous skin"
- "Repairs / rebuilds / restores tissue"
- "Clinically proven to [any outcome]" when the proof is collagen-as-such, not an authorised co-ingredient
- "87% of users saw…" or any percentage-based outcome claim sourced from competitor copy or unverified studies
- "Reduces hair loss" / "stops thinning" / "helps regrow"
- Any framing implying the product treats, prevents, or alleviates a condition (these become medicinal claims, a separate and stricter regime)
- Before/after photos that imply any of the above outcomes

These exact constructions appear on competitor sites including sunnasupplements.com. **Their presence on a competitor site is not evidence of legality.** Several halal-collagen competitors are operating in tension with the Regulation; we are not.

### What you CAN say about collagen (factual, structural)

You can describe what the product *is* without making a health claim about what it *does*. The line is at "function/health outcome." Permitted constructions include:

- "Hydrolysed marine collagen peptides"
- "Sourced from [named fish, e.g. wild-caught cod / tilapia]"
- "Type I collagen"
- "[X]g per serving"
- "Naturally occurring amino acids: glycine, proline, hydroxyproline"
- "[X]% protein content"
- "Bioavailable peptide profile, average molecular weight [Y] Daltons" (only if true and on the COA)
- "Halal-certified by [named body], certificate [number], valid until [date]" (only when current and on file — see `halal-certification.md`)
- "Fish allergen — see allergen panel" (mandatory)
- "Mixes easily, neutral taste" (sensory, not health)
- "Free from gluten, dairy, soy, added sugar, artificial sweeteners" (only if true)

The agent should also feel free to describe the *category* in educational, non-promotional terms ("Collagen is a structural protein found in skin, cartilage and bone") on blog content and the About page — but it cannot tie that description to a benefit claim for the product itself.

## 2. Co-ingredients that DO carry authorised claims

Because collagen carries no authorised health claims of its own, the brand's compliant benefit story must rest on co-ingredients with authorised claims. Use these in formulations where the brand genuinely wants to support the corresponding claim, and at the dose required by EU rules (the source-of-vitamin/mineral threshold from the Annex to Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, typically 15% of the Nutrient Reference Value per 100g or per portion as specified).

**The exact authorised wording is what matters.** What follows is taken from Regulation (EU) 432/2012 and the EU Register. Quote it. Do not improve it. Do not soften it. Do not strengthen it.

### Vitamin C (the workhorse for a beauty-positioned collagen product)

Most relevant to a skin-positioned product. Authorised claims include:

- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of bones."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of cartilage."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of gums."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of teeth."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of blood vessels."
- "Vitamin C contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress."
- "Vitamin C contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue."
- "Vitamin C contributes to normal function of the immune system."
- "Vitamin C increases iron absorption."

> **The skin/cartilage/bone/gum/teeth set is the legal foundation of a beauty-positioned halal collagen product in the EU.** Read that sentence again. This is how a compliant brand says "supports skin" — via the authorised vitamin-C claim, not via a forbidden collagen claim.

**Conditions of use:** the product must be at least a "source of vitamin C" — typically ≥ 12 mg per 100 g, per 100 ml, or per portion (check the Annex; "source" and "high in" thresholds differ). The claim attaches to the *vitamin C* in the product, not to the collagen.

### Biotin (for hair/nails/skin support)

- "Biotin contributes to the maintenance of normal hair."
- "Biotin contributes to the maintenance of normal skin."
- "Biotin contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes."
- "Biotin contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism."
- "Biotin contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system."
- "Biotin contributes to normal macronutrient metabolism."
- "Biotin contributes to normal psychological function."

**Notable absence:** EFSA did NOT authorise a "maintenance of normal nails" claim for biotin standalone (the application failed on substantiation grounds). For nails, see zinc and selenium below.

### Zinc (broad support — hair, nails, skin)

- "Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal hair."
- "Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal nails."
- "Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal skin."
- "Zinc contributes to normal cognitive function."
- "Zinc contributes to the normal function of the immune system."
- "Zinc contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress."
- "Zinc contributes to normal DNA synthesis."
- "Zinc contributes to normal protein synthesis."
- "Zinc has a role in the process of cell division."

### Selenium (nails, hair, immune)

- "Selenium contributes to the maintenance of normal hair."
- "Selenium contributes to the maintenance of normal nails."
- "Selenium contributes to the normal function of the immune system."
- "Selenium contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress."
- "Selenium contributes to normal thyroid function."

### Copper (hair pigmentation, connective tissue)

- "Copper contributes to the maintenance of normal connective tissues."
- "Copper contributes to normal hair pigmentation."
- "Copper contributes to normal skin pigmentation."
- "Copper contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress."

### Vitamin A (skin, mucous membranes)

- "Vitamin A contributes to the maintenance of normal skin."
- "Vitamin A contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes."
- "Vitamin A contributes to the maintenance of normal vision."

### Vitamin E (oxidative stress)

- "Vitamin E contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress."

### Riboflavin (skin, vision)

- "Riboflavin contributes to the maintenance of normal skin."
- "Riboflavin contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes."
- "Riboflavin contributes to the maintenance of normal vision."

### Niacin (skin)

- "Niacin contributes to the maintenance of normal skin."
- "Niacin contributes to the maintenance of normal mucous membranes."

> **Reminder.** Every claim above is conditional on the product meeting the minimum "source of [nutrient]" threshold per portion, as set out in the Annex to Regulation 1924/2006. Below that threshold the claim is not legally available, no matter how it is worded. The Email Marketing and Store Ops agents must not output any of these claims unless the formulation tab in the product master data confirms the threshold is met.

## 3. Constructing on-brand copy from authorised claims

This is where the brand's transparency-first positioning becomes a *feature*, not a constraint.

### The compliant pattern for a skin-positioned collagen product

The product page or ad does NOT say "marine collagen for glowing skin." It says, roughly:

> Hydrolysed marine collagen peptides with added vitamin C. **Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin.**

That second sentence is doing all the legal work. The first sentence is descriptive (what it is). The two together let a reader connect the dots without the brand making a forbidden claim.

### Allowed copy moves

- **Quote the authorised claim in full**, near the ingredient that earns it.
- **Describe what the product is** (composition, source, halal status when documented, allergens, taste, format).
- **Describe how the product is used** (one scoop daily, mix into coffee, etc.) — neutrally.
- **Tell the brand story** (founder, sourcing, certification process, why marine, why halal-by-source rather than by-slaughter) — without attaching outcome claims to the product.
- **Educational content** about what collagen *is* and *does in the body generally*, kept in editorial contexts (blog, About) and not framed as a benefit of *this product*.

### Disallowed copy moves

- Implying a benefit by juxtaposition. "Marine collagen for radiant skin" is a forbidden claim even though it doesn't use a verb — adjacency creates the link.
- "Beauty-from-within," "youth elixir," "anti-aging," "rejuvenating," "skin-loving," and similar evocative framings that mean the same thing as a forbidden claim. Same meaning = same claim = same problem.
- "Many of our customers report…" — testimonial-shaped claims are still claims.
- Comparative claims ("more bioavailable than ordinary collagen") unless they meet the strict comparative-claims rules in Article 9 of the Regulation and are substantiated.
- Stars, ratings, badges, or "as seen in" graphics that imply efficacy.
- Influencer or affiliate content that the brand has commissioned and that makes any forbidden claim — the brand is responsible for what its paid voices say.

## 4. Ingredients with NO authorised health claims

If the formulation contains any of the following — common in the supplement category, often borrowed by collagen brands as filler "actives" — they may NOT be the subject of any health or function claim:

- **Hyaluronic acid** — no authorised oral claim. Frequently abused in beauty supplement copy.
- **MSM (methylsulfonylmethane)** — no authorised claim.
- **Silica / silicon (as ingredient, not as nutrient)** — claims rejected by EFSA.
- **Bamboo extract** — no authorised claim.
- **Horsetail extract** — no authorised claim.
- **Aloe vera** — no authorised oral claim for skin.
- **Glucosamine / chondroitin** — claims rejected.
- **Pearl powder / mother of pearl** — no authorised claim.
- **Astaxanthin** — no authorised claim.
- **Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)** — no authorised claim.

These ingredients can appear on the ingredient list. They cannot drive a benefit claim, anywhere, in any wording.

## 5. The "general functioning" and "well-being" trap

Article 10(3) of the Regulation allows general, non-specific benefits-of-health references only when **accompanied by a specific authorised health claim from the permitted list, on the same packaging or in the same communication**. So:

- "For your daily wellness routine" ❌ alone — ✅ if immediately accompanied by an authorised claim ("…contains vitamin C, which contributes to the normal function of the immune system").
- "Take care of your skin from within" ❌ alone — ✅ if accompanied by the vitamin-C-and-skin claim above.

The agent must NOT publish a general well-being reference without a specific authorised claim attached. Pair them or drop the well-being line.

## 6. Special restrictions

### Medicinal / disease claims — never

Under EU Directive 2001/83/EC, anything that **presents the product as having properties for treating or preventing disease** is a medicinal claim and is forbidden for a food supplement, regardless of how true it might be. This includes:

- "Helps with arthritis / osteoporosis / eczema / psoriasis / acne"
- "Treats / cures / prevents / heals / fixes"
- "Anti-inflammatory" (in a therapeutic sense)
- "Hormonal support" / "hormone-balancing"
- "Detox / detoxifies / cleanses"
- Any reference to medication, prescriptions, or doctor recommendations specific to the product

Medical questions in customer support route to a human. The Customer Support agent NEVER drafts a medical reply, even a careful one.

### Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children

Avoid copy that recommends or markets the product to pregnant or breastfeeding women, or to children under 18, without explicit regulatory review. The product is not generally appropriate to market to these groups, and claims in these contexts are tightly restricted. Customer service questions on these topics escalate to a human, full stop.

### Allergens

Mandatory under Regulation (EU) 1169/2011:

- **Marine collagen contains fish.** "Contains fish" must appear on the label and in any product description that lists allergens online. Use "fish" not "marine" for the allergen line — the legal allergen vocabulary is specific.
- The cross-contamination disclosure (shared facility / shared equipment) is required if applicable per the manufacturer's COA.

### Halal certification claims

See `/skills/compliance/halal-certification.md`. The short version: "halal-certified" is only sayable when there is a current certificate on file from a recognised body, naming the SKU and batch range. The agent REFUSES to draft this language otherwise.

## 7. Decision tree (for the agent at draft time)

When an agent is about to produce any sentence that touches a benefit, run this in order:

1. **Does the sentence describe an outcome of the product itself (skin, hair, nails, joints, energy, mood, anything)?**
   - If no → it's descriptive, you can write it (subject to the rest of the file).
   - If yes → go to 2.

2. **Is the outcome attributable to a co-ingredient with an authorised claim, at a dose that meets the "source of" threshold?**
   - If yes → use the exact authorised wording from §2, attached to the ingredient (not to "the product" or "the collagen").
   - If no → go to 3.

3. **Is the outcome on the forbidden list (§1, §4, §6)?**
   - If yes → REFUSE. Route to `#approvals` with a flag. Do not output the sentence even in a draft.
   - If unsure → REFUSE. Route to `#approvals` with a flag. The bias is hard toward refusal.

4. **Would a regulator, reading this sentence in isolation, treat it as a health claim?**
   - If yes → it is one, regardless of how it's framed. Same rules apply.

If the agent successfully constructs a benefit-shaped sentence anchored to an authorised claim, the agent MUST also verify (from product master data) that the product actually meets the dosage threshold for the claim. If it doesn't, the claim is not available and the sentence is REFUSED.

## 8. What to do when the founder (or a competitor's example) pushes back

The founder may at some point say "but Sunna says X, why can't we?" or "competitor Y is doing it." This is a known pattern and the agent handles it the same way every time:

1. Acknowledge the point. Confirm that yes, competitor copy often makes claims that are not compliant.
2. Restate the brand's positioning: trust is the entire premise; compliance is the proof of trust. The brand specifically does not want to be in the position the incumbents are in.
3. Offer the compliant equivalent: "We can't say [X], but we can say [authorised wording], which lands very close and is defensible."
4. If the founder insists on shipping the non-compliant claim anyway, the agent REFUSES to draft it and routes to `#approvals` with a flag. The founder can override in `#approvals` with full visibility — the override is logged in `agent_actions` and the agent is no longer the publisher of record.

## 9. Update discipline

This file is a working draft of public regulation, not the regulation itself.

- The EU Register changes. New claims get authorised; some get re-evaluated. This file should be reviewed against the EU Register at least every six months, and immediately when the brand introduces a new ingredient or co-ingredient.
- Country-specific guidance (NL: NVWA; DE: BVL; FR: DGCCRF) sometimes adds national-level positions on top of EU rules. The active-market file (`markets/nl.md`) is the place to record any NL-specific overlay.
- Brexit means the UK is now governed by the UK Nutrition and Health Claims Register (under the FSA), which is similar but not identical. The UK claim set lives in `markets/uk.md` and is not interchangeable with this file.
- The US (FDA) uses a different framework entirely (structure/function claims under DSHEA, with the standard "These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA…" disclaimer). The US claim set lives in `markets/us.md` and is not interchangeable with this file.

Any change to this file must be made by a human, reviewed by a qualified regulatory consultant or the cert body's regulatory advisor, and logged in `_corrections_log.md` with the date and reason.
